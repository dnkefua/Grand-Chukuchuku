# Grand-Chukuchuku — Chapter One Writers' Room v1

**Chapter:** Fiango
**Format:** 12 episodes, 2 minutes 15 seconds to 2 minutes 45 seconds each
**Tone:** Hard-R animated coming-of-age thriller
**Purpose:** This is the room's working map, not final biography. Each episode must distinguish confirmed memory, family testimony, reported event, and proposed composite before a shooting script is written.

## The chapter promise

Fiango is not a list of hardships. It is the place where Zino learns three formative lessons: being smart does not solve a money problem; performing confidence is different from feeling safe; and what a crowd calls justice can be terrifyingly unjust.

The chapter begins with a ten-year-old boy who creates Grand-Chukuchuku because he needs an impossible hero. It ends in 2007 with a young man who has completed secondary school but still cannot promise his mother that talent will pay for the next stage of his life.

## Character engines

- **Zino:** Wants success, approval, justice, and eventually a house for his mother. He needs to learn that cleverness, performance, and pleasing others cannot solve every crisis. Grand-Chukuchuku is his rehearsal for power.
- **Mother:** Protects through education while holding a household together on a primary-school teacher's income. She needs humor, boundaries, professional pride, and decisions of her own; she cannot exist only to sacrifice.
- **Gideon:** Sees opportunity early. His camera is a tool and a symbol of investment. His money and strategy complement Zino's social confidence, creating a friendship with generosity and imbalance.
- **Luc:** Uses faith, discipline, and direct outreach to make sense of people. Zino helps him with maths and confidence; Luc can identify the truth Zino wants to avoid. His faith must be tested after Episode 9.
- **Miss Mukete:** Represents recognition beyond the family. She can set a standard for Zino without becoming an invented rescuer.
- **Elianne:** Must have a goal, agency, and an actual chronology before she is scripted as Zino's first love.
- **Father as absence:** He died when Zino was two. Use family testimony, an object, a photograph, or conflicting adult accounts—not a Zino flashback.

## Culture and visual rules

- The compound is a pressure cooker: two families, three single mothers, two bachelors, and three student tenants share a well, mango tree, rent anxiety, music, food, gossip, and the landlord's monthly visit.
- The first five years are Fiango day school with Luc and Gideon. Professional School is a daytime vocational campus for building, construction, and electrical training. Around June, its rooms open at night for day-school exam study. It holds genuine work and distraction; never treat all students as delinquent.
- The final two years are at a remote boarding high school reached after a day of travel through mountains and valleys. It brings top students, wealthy parents, white teachers and priests, strict discipline, bullying, and a distance from home that changes Zino's sense of belonging.
- Zino shifts language by relationship: Pidgin with friends, English at home, French and English in school. Use remembered phrases rather than generic slang.
- Jungle justice is direct hard-R material. The accused person's guilt stays unknown. The crowd's certainty is the horror. Show the violence through Zino's witness perspective, then show the change it leaves in him.

## Required fixes before shooting scripts

1. Give the finale a concrete post-secondary financial barrier, deadline, or opportunity from Zino's memory.
2. Establish a consistent rule for Grand-Chukuchuku: every fantasy reveals something Zino cannot say plainly; after “The Crowd,” the device should fail or change.
3. Confirm the dates and details of the day-school fee exclusion, boarding-school fence punishments Zino witnessed, and the jungle-justice composite.
4. Record the day-school name, boarding-school name and start year, travel route, Professional School years, Miss Mukete's period, and Elianne's period.
5. Give the mother a personal desire and a boundary that creates dramatic choice.
6. Track recurring objects: football, landlord notebook, well bucket, mango tree, exercise book, Gideon's camera, Luc's Bible, and the school fence. Each should mean something different by Episode 12.

## Critic's nonnegotiables

1. The Chapter One finale must leave Zino facing an irreversible, concrete question about funding the next stage of education.
2. The mother must make choices, set limits, want things, and sometimes complicate Zino's plan; she is not a symbol of suffering.
3. Father material must remain inherited family history, not a false first-person memory.
4. Grand-Chukuchuku requires clear rules and must change after Zino witnesses what imagination cannot solve.
5. Gideon's hustling and Luc's faith each need costs and tests of their own.
6. Comic danger, humiliation, and hard-R violence must feel different; “The Crowd” should rupture the earlier rhythm.
7. Recurring objects must gather meaning rather than remain decorative set dressing.
8. Every shooting script must list its fact tier so dramatization never silently becomes autobiography.

## Fact tiers

**Confirmed:** father died when Zino was two; mother was a primary-school teacher; Gideon and Luc's core traits; compound arrangement; monthly rent and electricity collection; five day-school years with Luc and Gideon; two remote boarding high-school years; Professional School identity; at least four jungle-justice incidents during day school; school milestones.

**Unresolved:** exact calendar dates, school names, individual jungle-justice events, individual fee-exclusion details, the boarding-school fence punishments, the father's family testimony, and Elianne's chronology.

**Proposed composite:** exact dialogue, landlord visit in Episode 1, the single jungle-justice event in Episode 9, Gideon lowering the camera, Luc losing words, and the mother's immediate response.
