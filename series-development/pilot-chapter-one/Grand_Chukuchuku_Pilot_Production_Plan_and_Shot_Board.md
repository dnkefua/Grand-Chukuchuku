# Grand Chukuchuku — Two-Minute Pilot Production Plan

Working plan for creator review • No new generations or credits used in preparing this plan.

## Purpose and non-negotiables

Make a two-minute, three-act animated pilot that begins with Zino, Gideon and Luc meeting at the roadside during the holiday after primary school. All three will attend GBHS Kumba. Luc would have preferred SASSE, but his parents cannot afford it; this is story context, not a newly invented line.

The road separates the established family compound from Professional School. Match the approved compound: its gate, surrounding rooms, central mango tree and well. Keep passersby, bikes and cars in a living neighborhood; at least three other children actively play whenever Zino plays with the ball, and play pauses for passing people and traffic. Gideon and Luc watch rather than play.

Zino wears the same dark low-top canvas shoes with light soles in every shot. The three boys have comparable early-secondary-school age and scale. Keep every named character visually consistent, and keep Zino’s mother distinct from every other tenant. No appearance, portrait, silhouette, image or fantasy of Grand Chukuchuku. The landlord is authoritative and feared because tenants owe rent; the arrival is tense, not happy.

The pilot ends with Zino warning his mother and the landlord arriving at the compound gate, before a response or confrontation. The next episode starts on the first school day. The four tenant/child lines below remain proposed, pending the creator’s approval; preserve all author-edited wording exactly.

## Timing and editorial geography

Act I: 00:00–00:43, excitement and the meeting. Act II: 00:43–01:23, school hopes, Luc’s quiet disappointment, then the first hint of dread. Act III: 01:23–02:00, the landlord’s approach and unresolved gate arrival.

The working script contains 212 spoken words: 85 in Act I, 87 in Act II and 40 in Act III. At 150 words per minute the text alone occupies about 85 seconds, leaving about 35 seconds for pauses, reactions and traffic across the whole film. Three short, separately marked improvised options add 16 words if all are used, making about 228 spoken words, or roughly 91 seconds at that hypothetical speed. Actual Pidgin delivery and pauses may differ. Perform a complete table read and use the options only where there is space; Act II is tight. If it does not fit, discuss exact changes rather than cutting or rewriting the author’s lines silently.

Adopt a provisional screen-direction rule using the approved reference: view from the Professional School side toward the compound, with the road between them; on this axis Zino is generally left, Gideon center and Luc right. Stay on one side of the conversation axis; Zino travels from the road toward the compound when warning his mother. Confirm the precise real layout against the approved plan before locking this rule. Never substitute the school gate for the compound gate.

Use eye-level child-centered camera work, calm motivated moves, restrained depth of field, warm neighborhood color at the start and a gradual shift to tense contrast. Lens values are full-frame-equivalent visual guides for a 2D scene, not physical camera requirements. No repeated aerial establishing shot, unexplained whip pans or superhero imagery.

## Locked characters, wardrobe and props

Create separate single-character reference assets, not only a group sheet: Zino (white vest, blue shorts and the same dark shoes), Gideon (green top and distinctive hair), Luc (yellow top and high flat-top), Zino’s mother, the landlord, and each recurring tenant. Keep face, hairstyle, age, height, skin tone and clothing stable in front, profile and three-quarter views. The currently available group sheet is an identity guide, not a finished scene or an automatic guarantee.

Use a cast roster for at least six different adult tenants: W1, W2, W3, M1, M2 and M3, each with distinct face, body, age, outfit and silhouette. The IDs are production placeholders, not canonical names. Assign one reference per recurring person and never reuse the mother’s face for a tenant or M3’s design for the landlord.

Create one authoritative clean compound background, the separate Professional School frontage, an overhead blocking diagram for production use, separate foreground objects, and a gate with no Professional School sign. Add any school sign only to its correct building in post. The landlord carries a ledger with tenant names and negative rent amounts written in red. Until the creator confirms names and amounts, prop entries are temporary. Render clean prop pages and composite legible labels in editing if necessary.

## Temporary teenage voices and later recordings

Cast three distinct teenage-sounding male scratch voices, without claiming a Cameroonian accent. Zino: bright, quick, energetic mid-high voice; turns urgent when he spots the landlord. Gideon: warm, slightly lower and playful voice; steady and teasing. Luc: soft, thoughtful, clearly different voice; subdued on the SASSE line. Audition the same short line length for each, check they are distinguishable without pictures, and save each selected voice ID and settings.

Use a separate adult voice for each speaking tenant and a distinct child voice. Record or synthesize each line as its own clean file. The original Pidgin text stays unchanged; keep pronunciation notes separate and listen with the creator, because an AI voice may mispronounce it. Do not silently translate or replace words. Create a temporary dialogue edit before animating; review rhythm, clarity and total runtime.

OpenArt’s website advertises Create Voice-over and Lip-Sync. Its current connector in this chat does not expose a direct voice-over or standalone lip-sync method; execution may require the OpenArt interface or another approved tool. Confirm the available teenage voice catalog, animated-character support and exact cost with one shot before committing. The Lip-Sync page advertises uploading an image or video and 3–60 seconds of audio, but success for these animated characters has not yet been tested.

For the later Cameroonian-accent pass, the creator can record each actor’s actual complete lines, ideally separate 48 kHz WAV files with room tone and no music. Replace the scratch dialogue stems and redo lip sync wherever phonemes or timing change; rebuild timing if natural delivery needs it. A short accent sample can guide casting, but it is not a substitute for full final line recordings. Clone a voice only with the speaker’s permission.

## Production workflow and quality gates

1. Freeze the author-approved script and resolve proposed tenant lines. Read it aloud with temporary voices and a stopwatch; preserve the exact 120-second runtime only if the performances breathe naturally. If the read is long, propose exact optional trims for the creator rather than compressing or changing the dialogue.

2. Lock the set, individual character sheets, shoe design, cast roster, ledger artwork and screen direction. Create a separate approved 16:9 start-frame still for every shot, with the actual scene composition and no model-sheet panels. Review the entire board against the location reference and approve only once all signs and character positions are correct.

3. Assemble a timed animatic from those stills, the separate scratch dialogue, placeholder ambience and temporary edits. Check the whole 2:00 story, the bike pause, the tenant reactions and the final compound-gate cliffhanger before buying video renders.

4. Run a small paid proof on ONE dialogue shot with a single visible speaker and approved start frame; check mouth movement against the actual scratch audio. Separately test ONE crowd/traffic shot. Stop after each test and inspect shoes, faces, player count, camera move, background, lip sync and sound. If a test fails, diagnose it or change the workflow; do not launch a full batch on a failed proof.

5. For each approved shot, animate its specific scene still with OpenArt image-to-video when the shot should start from that exact frame. Use a short one-action, one-camera-move prompt; disable prompt rewriting if available. Use element/reference-to-video only when creating a new composition from character/location references, and check a still first. Do not use an entire multi-panel storyboard sheet as the literal video start frame. Generate clean visuals and maintain separate dialogue and effects tracks.

6. Lip-sync one visible speaker at a time. Where a shot includes multiple speakers, use approved reverse shots, reactions or off-screen dialogue; generate extra subshots if required. Review the full-resolution result because a higher-resolution rerender can change the performance. Composite correct gate signs and ledger figures in the editor; do not rely on AI text to be accurate.

7. Edit the approved shots in sequence with motivated hard cuts, brief sound bridges and consistent screen direction. Use separate dialogue, ambience, effects and optional music stems. Mix the ball, bikes, cars, footsteps, doors, keys and ledger pages intentionally. Keep the neighborhood audible under the landlord tension without burying speech.

8. Run a shot-by-shot acceptance review, then export a two-minute review draft. Inspect beginning and ending, every identity and pair of shoes, every player count, all signs, red debt entries, traffic pauses, landlord dread, all dialogue and lip sync. Correct before calling the draft ready. Retain the full editable project and every approved source.

## Fallback and realistic schedule

If an opening shot or a lip-sync test fails twice on the same issue, stop paying for blind rerolls. Change the approach: use separate approved character layers and backgrounds, controlled 2D rigging or hand animation, then composite traffic, readable text and effects in the edit. A human animation artist may be needed for exact body movement and expressions. AI-generated motion can still be used for individual background or insert shots where it passes review.

For one skilled producer/editor, a planning estimate after the script and assets are available is roughly half a day to two days to lock boards and assets; half a day to one and a half days for scratch voices and an animatic; half a day to one day for the proof; two to five or more days for shot production; and half a day to two days for sound, edits and QC. This is an illustrative 4–10+ working days, not a guarantee. Queue times, creator feedback, credit availability, character failures and a hand-animated fallback can extend it. A coordinated crew can work in parallel, but higher production quality still requires review and iteration.
